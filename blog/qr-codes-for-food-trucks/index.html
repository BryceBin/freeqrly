QR Codes for Food Trucks: One Menu Board, Every Job Covered

A food truck's menu, location, and queue change every day, so its QR codes have to change with them. This guide sets up a small set of codes — one for each job — that keeps the printed board true, shortens the queue, and brings customers back to the next pitch.

November 4, 2026 · 15 min read

Why a food truck needs a different QR plan

A restaurant sits still. A food truck does not. Its menu changes when a supplier runs out, its location changes by the day, and its customers often meet it for the first time while standing in line. That is why a food truck benefits more from QR codes than a fixed restaurant does, and why it also punishes a careless setup more quickly. A code that points to yesterday's menu, last week's pitch, or a dead ordering page wastes the one moment a passer-by was ready to buy. The good news is that the whole system can be built from a handful of codes, each with one job and one clear label, and most of them never need to be reprinted.

The trick is to separate what changes from what does not. Anything that changes — today's menu, today's location, today's prices, this week's special — belongs behind a code you can update after printing. Anything that will stay true for months or years — your main website, a permanent contact page — can be a simple code printed once and left alone. Get that split right and the rest is placement, size, and a short sentence telling people what they get when they scan.

Static or dynamic: the decision to make before you print

A static code stores its destination directly in the printed pattern. A dynamic code stores a short redirect instead, so the destination behind it can be changed later without changing the printed pattern. That difference is the whole decision. If the destination might change, print a dynamic code. If it will not change and you do not need scan counts, a static code is simpler and safer, because it depends on nothing but the pattern and the destination itself.

The daily menu that never goes stale

This is the highest-value code on a food truck. Print one menu code on the board, on the side of the truck, and on the queue sign, and point it at a page you control: your own menu page, your ordering platform's menu, or a simple page you update each morning. When an item sells out, when a special replaces it, or when a price moves, you edit the page or the dynamic code's destination. The printed board stays the same; the menu customers see is today's.

Keep the page itself ruthlessly practical. Put the day's items first, mark sold-out items clearly rather than deleting them mid-service, and show prices, allergens, and dietary notes in plain language. A customer in a queue should be able to decide in under a minute, phone in one hand, without pinching, zooming, or downloading a file. If your menu lives as a PDF, use a dynamic code so you can swap the file behind it. The failure to avoid is a code that points to a screenshot or a social media post that slides down the feed, where customers cannot find it again.

Location and schedule: answer where are you today before anyone asks

For a moving business, the most common question is also the most expensive to answer one person at a time: where are you today, and when are you there? Point one dynamic code at your current schedule page — this week's pitches, hours, and any last-minute change — and put it everywhere a new customer might find you after you have left: packaging, flyers, and the truck itself.

Write the schedule page for a stranger, not a regular. Lead with today and tomorrow, give the place name and a recognisable landmark as well as the address, and state hours plainly, including when you stop serving. If a pitch is cancelled or moved, update that page first, rather than leaving the old pitch on the page and hoping a social post reaches everyone. Do not promise live tracking you do not provide, and do not link the code to a map pin for a single past pitch, because that pin will still look authoritative long after you have driven away.

Ordering ahead: shorten the queue without rushing the food

A queue is good news until it starts turning people away. An order-ahead code lets customers browse, choose, and pay through your existing ordering platform while they wait, or before they arrive, and then collect at the window. The code itself does not take the order; it removes the steps between a hungry person and the ordering page you already use. Print it at the back of the queue, not only at the window, so people use the waiting time to decide.

Set expectations on the ordering page and on the sign: how pickup works — order number, name, or a message when ready — and how long the wait is at busy times. Keep window ordering open and present the code as the faster option, not the only option. Test the whole journey yourself on mobile data, not just on the truck's WiFi.

Loyalty without an app nobody wants to install

Loyalty works for a food truck when it asks for almost nothing. A code that opens a simple sign-up — an email or phone form, a stamp-card page, or your existing loyalty platform — can turn a one-time visitor into a repeat customer without forcing an app download in the middle of a queue. Keep the reward simple, state it on the sign, and collect only what you need to recognise the customer next time. Buy nine, get the tenth free is easy to understand at a glance; a points scheme that needs an explanation is not.

Place the loyalty code where the customer is already satisfied and unhurried: at the pickup window, on the receipt, or on the bag.

Google reviews at the moment customers are happiest

The best time to ask for a review is when the food is in the customer's hands and they are pleased with it. A code that opens your Google review form directly — not a search results page, not your homepage — turns that moment into a single scan. Get the direct review link from your Google Business Profile, turn it into a code, and place it where a satisfied customer is already standing: the pickup window, the receipt, and the takeaway bag.

Ask everyone the same way, once, and do not offer anything in exchange for a review. Never offer a discount, a free item, or entry into a draw for posting a review, and never ask only the customers you expect to be happy. Review platforms treat incentives and selective asking as manipulation, and a review profile built only from hand-picked happy customers is easy for readers to spot. One visible code plus one spoken, voluntary ask is enough, and then silence.

If each location has its own Business Profile, make one code per location and label your printed files by placement. For the full steps to find the review link, see our guide to getting more Google reviews with a QR code.

Catering bookings: turn a festival crowd into future work

Festivals, markets, and private events are where catering enquiries happen, and they happen in a hurry. A catering code that opens a short enquiry form — date, location, approximate guest numbers, and a contact detail — lets an organiser capture the idea while your food is still fresh in their mind. Keep the form short enough to finish on a phone in a crowd; every extra field is an enquiry you will never receive.

Print the catering code separately from the menu code, with its own label: Catering and private events — scan to enquire. Use a dynamic code here so that if your booking form moves, the printed cards still lead to the right place, and say on the form when you will reply.

On the truck versus on packaging: same code, different job

A code on the truck and a code on packaging look identical, but they work at different moments. The truck code is scanned by someone standing in front of you, with a decision to make now. The packaging code is scanned later, at home or at work, by someone who has already eaten and may want to find you again, leave a review, or book you. Give each moment its own code and its own label, even if the truck carries several codes at once.

On the truck, prioritise the menu and order-ahead codes, large enough to read from the queue. On packaging, prioritise the review code, the schedule page, and the loyalty sign-up, because those are the actions a customer takes after the meal. A small code on a cup or bag still needs the same basics: at least 2 × 2 cm in print, dark on light, with a clear margin, and a label that says what it does. An unlabelled code on packaging is decoration; a labelled one is a second visit.

Print and placement basics: size, quiet zone, and weather

Outdoor codes fail for ordinary, fixable reasons: too small for the distance, a crowded margin, low contrast, glare, or a surface that has faded, peeled, or got wet. Start with size. As a rule of thumb, never print smaller than 2 × 2 cm, and make the code at least one-tenth as wide as the distance people will scan from. A card scanned from 50 cm needs a code around 5 cm wide; a poster scanned from 5 metres needs one around 50 cm wide. When in doubt, print larger: a bigger code costs almost nothing, while a reprint costs the whole run. The full tables are in our QR code print size guide.

Leave the quiet zone alone. That is the blank margin around the code, at least four modules — four of the code's own little squares — wide on all four sides, in the same light colour as the code's background. Text, borders, logos, and the edge of the sign must stay outside it, or the phone may never find the edges of the pattern. Keep the code dark on a light background, avoid placing it over a photograph or a busy pattern, and keep the three large corner squares uncovered, because the scanner uses them to orient the pattern.

For outdoor use, choose materials for the weather they will actually meet. As practical guidance, use vinyl or laminated stock for signs that stay on the truck, a matte finish where sunlight or spotlights cause glare, and a flat panel rather than a curved surface, which distorts the grid. One vendor of outdoor vinyl stickers describes its own product as waterproof, scratch-resistant, and UV-protected with a protective laminate — the kind of specification to look for, not a promise to assume for every printer. Print a proof at final size on the final material, scan it with more than one phone, in daylight and in dim light, from the real distance and angle, and check it again after rain and cleaning. If a code scans sometimes but not always, treat it as broken.

Export for print properly. Use SVG, which scales without blur, or a high-resolution PNG, and never stretch a code out of proportion or save it as a low-quality JPEG, which blurs the sharp module edges a scanner depends on. Keep the original export file, labelled by placement, so a damaged sign can be reprinted exactly. If a printed code stops scanning and you cannot find the fault, work through size, contrast, quiet zone, damage, light, and destination in that order — the full checklist is in our QR code troubleshooting guide.

A starter set you can print this week

You do not need a code for everything on day one. Start with four: a dynamic menu code, a dynamic schedule code, a review code, and an order-ahead code if you already use an ordering platform. Add loyalty and catering codes once those four are working and labelled well. Four codes, each with one job, will serve a food truck better than a dozen codes nobody can tell apart.

Before a big print run, run a simple preflight: scan every code from the real distance, on two phones, on mobile data; confirm each destination opens on a phone; check the quiet zone on the finished artwork; and keep a spare of each sign. Good to know

Should my food truck menu QR code be static or dynamic?

Dynamic, in almost every case. A daily menu, its prices, and its sold-out items change too often for a fixed code. A dynamic code lets you update the destination after printing, so the printed board never goes stale.

What happens if my menu page moves after I have printed the code?

If the code is dynamic, edit its destination in your dashboard and every printed copy follows. If it is static, the printed pattern still points to the old address, so you must reprint or restore the old page.

How big should a QR code be on a food truck?

As a rule of thumb, never print smaller than 2 by 2 cm, and make the code at least one-tenth as wide as the scanning distance. A card scanned from 50 cm needs a code around 5 cm wide, and a poster scanned from 5 metres needs one around 50 cm wide. When in doubt, print larger.

What is the quiet zone, and why does it matter outdoors?

The quiet zone is the blank margin around the code, at least four modules wide on all four sides, in the same light colour as the background. Scanners use it to find the edges of the pattern, so text, borders, or a tight crop running into it can stop a perfect-looking code from scanning.

Where should I put the Google review code on a food truck?

At the pickup window, on the receipt, and on the takeaway bag — where a satisfied customer is already standing with the food in hand. Ask everyone the same way, once, and never offer anything in exchange for a review.

Can one QR code handle the menu, ordering, reviews, and catering?

It can point to a single homepage, but that forces customers to hunt for the right page, and most will not. Give each job its own code and its own label: menu, order ahead, reviews, loyalty, and catering work best as separate, clearly labelled codes.

How do I keep a truck code scannable in rain and sun?

Print on vinyl or laminated stock, use a matte finish where glare is a risk, keep the code flat rather than curved, and proof it at final size on the final material in daylight and dim light. Check it again after rain and cleaning, and treat a code that scans only sometimes as broken.

Do I need an app for food truck loyalty or ordering QR codes?

No. The code simply opens a web page or form the customer already knows how to use — an ordering page, a sign-up form, or a stamp-card page. Avoid forcing an app download in the middle of a queue.
