The Monday office workers scan the code on Wednesday and land on Monday's office-park address; the customer who loved the dumplings at the market drives to the office park on Thursday because that is where the flyer says the truck is — and the truck is at a brewery six miles away.

That is what a fixed printed code does when the business it advertises changes location every day. A restaurant can afford a printed code that points at one address forever; a food truck cannot, because the address is the one thing that is never the same twice. So the location question comes first: if a customer cannot find the truck today, nothing else matters.

What "dynamic" means when your address changes daily

A static code bakes its destination into the printed pattern: if the address changes, the pattern is wrong, and the only fix is a reprint. A dynamic code points at a short redirect you control — change the destination in your dashboard and every printed copy, on the truck, the bags, and last month's flyers, follows instantly.

For a fixed shop, that is a convenience; for a food truck, it is the difference between a sign that works and a sign that lies. The board on the truck can stay up for a year while the destination behind its code changes every morning — and again at lunchtime if a pitch is rained off.

Make it a morning routine: before the truck rolls out, spend two minutes checking that the schedule page leads with today, the menu marks sold-outs and shows the special, and the order-ahead code opens the right page. With a static code, the same morning means reprinting the board, the queue sign, and the flyers — or accepting that the print is wrong.

The "Find us today" code: the most important code on the truck

If you print only one code, print this one — it answers the question every food truck hears most: "Where are you today?"

Point a dynamic code at your schedule page — one page that always leads with today and tomorrow — and put it everywhere a customer might meet you after you have left: the truck side, the bag, the cup, the receipt, the flyer. The page has one job: answer three things in under five seconds for a stranger.

  • Where are you today, exactly? Place name, street address, and a landmark ("next to the library steps"). A postcode alone is not enough at a market, where five trucks share one.
  • When are you there? Opening time, closing time, and when you stop serving — often earlier than when you pack up. State both.
  • What if it changed? If a pitch is cancelled or rained off, the schedule page is the first thing you update, before any social post. The code on last week's flyer still leads here, so the flyer is never wrong.

Do not point this code at a map pin for a single past pitch: a pin looks authoritative long after you have driven away, and a customer who follows it to an empty car park will not give you a second chance. Do not point it at a social media profile either: a feed buries today's location under yesterday's photos within hours.

The schedule page also earns its keep on days you are not serving the public: Dumpling House's Saturday is a private wedding, and the page says so — "Closed to the public today, back at Lakeside Park on Sunday, 10:00–16:00." A closed day handled well is a customer kept.

The menu code that survives sold-outs

The menu code is the second code to print. It is not there to show a beautiful menu — it is there to show today's menu to a hungry person in a queue with one hand free and sixty seconds to decide. So the page behind the code matters more than the code itself:

  • Today's items first. The full menu can live further down, but the top is today: what is on, what is off, the special.
  • Sold out means marked, not deleted. When the pork belly runs out at 13:00, mark it "Sold out" rather than deleting it: deleting confuses the customer who saw it on the board five minutes ago; marking tells the truth and nudges them to the next choice.
  • Prices, allergens, and dietary notes in plain language. Put the price next to the item, every time — a queue is no place to guess whether the dumplings contain shellfish.
  • No downloads, no pinching, no sign-in. If your menu is a PDF, it must open in the browser and show the first page immediately, on a phone, on mobile data — if it forces a download, half your queue gives up. If it lives as a social media post, it slides down the feed and is unfindable by tomorrow. A page on your own site, or your ordering platform's menu page, is the right home, and a dynamic code lets you swap it without reprinting.

Do not print a separate menu code for every day of the week. One menu code, updated daily, beats seven codes nobody can tell apart.

Ordering from the queue: let waiting time do the work

A queue outside a food truck is good news until it starts turning people away. The person at the back of a twenty-person line is wondering how long this will take — and every minute without information makes leaving likelier. An order-ahead code gives them something useful to do with the wait: browse, choose, pay, and collect at the window when their name or number is called.

The code does not take the order; it removes the steps between a hungry person and the ordering page you use. So put it at the back of the queue, not only at the window — by the time a customer reaches the window, the ordering opportunity is gone. Put it on the queue sign, a standing board halfway down the line, and the menu board, with a label that promises the benefit: "Order ahead from the queue — skip the wait at the window."

Three details make or break order-ahead. Say how pickup works, on the sign and on the page — order number, name called out, or a text when ready; pick one and stick to it. Say how long the wait is, honestly: at busy times, put the current wait on the ordering page or the queue sign. Keep window ordering open: a customer without phone battery or mobile data, or who wants to talk to a human, should still be able to order at the window.

Test the whole journey yourself, on mobile data, from the back of your own queue — and at the rally, not just the quiet weekday pitch, where the queue is longest and the signal is worst.

Turning one visit into the next visit: loyalty and social follow

A food truck's hardest marketing problem is not the first visit. It is the second: the truck moves, the customer forgets, and next time they go to the place they can find. Loyalty and social follow codes close that gap.

Loyalty without an app. Nobody wants to install an app in the middle of a food queue, and a loyalty scheme that demands one will be ignored. A code that opens a simple sign-up — an email or phone form, a stamp-card page, or your existing loyalty platform — asks for almost nothing and gives the customer a reason to return. Keep the reward simple enough to state in one line: "Buy nine, get the tenth free." A points scheme that needs a paragraph of explanation is homework, not loyalty. Collect only what you need to recognise the customer next time, state the reward on the sign, and place the code where the customer is already satisfied and unhurried: the pickup window, the receipt, the bag — not the back of the queue.

Social follow, done once and done right. A code that opens your Instagram or TikTok profile has one job: turn a one-time eater into a follower who sees tomorrow's location post. Put it on the bag and the receipt, with a reason, not just a logo: "Follow us to see where we are tomorrow." Keep it to one platform per code — a page of six social icons forces a choice, and most will choose none.

Do not combine loyalty and social into one "follow and join" code. The customer who wants the loyalty stamp and the one who wants to follow you on Instagram are doing two different things, and one code serving both serves neither well.

Reviews and catering: the codes that work while you drive away

Both belong on packaging, because packaging is the only part of the truck that travels home with the customer.

Reviews at the moment of maximum happiness. The best time to ask is when the food is in the customer's hands and they are pleased with it. A code opening your Google review form directly turns that moment into one scan. Get the direct link from your Google Business Profile and place the code at the pickup window, on the receipt, and on the bag.

Ask everyone the same way, once. Never offer anything in exchange for a review, and never ask only the customers you expect to be happy — review platforms treat incentives and selective asking as manipulation. One visible code plus one spoken, voluntary ask is enough. A static code is fine: a Google review link rarely changes. Use dynamic if you want scan counts per placement.

Catering: turn a festival crowd into future work. Festivals and markets are where catering enquiries happen, and they happen in a hurry: an organiser tastes your food at a rally, has a company party in three weeks, and wants to capture the idea before the crowd carries them away. A catering code opening a short form — date, location, guest numbers, contact detail — lets them do it in under a minute, on a phone.

Keep the form short enough to finish standing up; every extra field is an enquiry you will never receive. Say when you will reply — "We reply within one working day" — and mean it. Print the catering code separately, labelled "Catering and private events — scan to enquire," as a dynamic code, so the printed cards keep working if your form moves. Put it where organisers stand: the festival banner, the business cards at the window, the flyers — not buried on the menu board.

Printing for a vehicle that lives outdoors

A food truck is a moving billboard that gets rained on, sun-bleached, and bumped by trolleys. Its codes fail for ordinary, fixable reasons: too small, a crowded margin, low contrast, glare, or a faded surface.

Size is a distance decision, not a design decision. As a rule of thumb, never print smaller than 2 by 2 cm, and make the code at least one-tenth as wide as the scanning distance: a cup code scanned from 50 cm needs to be around 5 cm wide; a truck-side code scanned from 5 metres, around 50 cm. When in doubt, print larger — a bigger code costs almost nothing, a reprint costs the whole run.

The quiet zone is not decoration. The blank margin around the code — at least four modules wide on all four sides, in the same light colour as the background — is how a phone finds the edges of the pattern. Text, borders, and logos must stay outside it, or a perfect-looking code will not scan. Keep the code dark on a light background, and keep the three corner squares uncovered.

Materials matter. Use vinyl or laminated stock, a matte finish where sunlight or spotlights cause glare, and a flat panel, not a curved surface. Proof it at final size on the final material, scan it with more than one phone in daylight and dim light, and check it again after rain and cleaning. A code that scans only sometimes is broken.

Export properly and keep the files: SVG, which scales without blur, or a high-resolution PNG — never a low-quality JPEG. Keep each export labelled by placement, so a damaged sign can be reprinted exactly. Good to know

Should my food truck's codes be static or dynamic?

Dynamic, for almost everything: the location, menu, prices, and specials change daily, and a dynamic code can be re-aimed after printing. Static codes are for things that never change, like your homepage. With FreeQrly, dynamic codes are free after sign-in — 20 on the free tier.

What happens if my truck moves after I have printed the code?

If the code is dynamic, nothing breaks: update the destination in your dashboard, or edit the schedule page, and every printed copy follows. If it is static, the pattern still points to the old address, so you must reprint the sign or restore the old page.

Can one QR code handle the menu, ordering, reviews, and catering?

It can point to one page linking to all of them, but a hungry person in a queue will not hunt for the right link. Give each job its own code and label.

Where should the order-ahead code go?

At the back of the queue, on the queue sign and a standing board halfway down the line. A code that only appears at the window arrives too late. Keep window ordering open too, so the code is the faster option, not the only option.

What should I check every morning before service?

Three things, in two minutes: the schedule page leads with today; the menu marks sold-outs and shows the special; the order-ahead code opens the right page.
