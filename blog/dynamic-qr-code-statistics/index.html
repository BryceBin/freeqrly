What dynamic QR statistics tell you

A dynamic code reports home every time it's scanned. Here's what FreeQrly counts, how to read it, and what it never collects.

October 16, 2026 · Last updated: November 3, 2026 · 15 min read

A static code is silent: it never reports home, so nobody — including you — knows how often it was scanned. A dynamic code reports every scan to your Dashboard. Here is what FreeQrly counts, how to read it, what it deliberately never collects, and how to use your own numbers to make decisions — plus the only outside numbers this post cites, each with its original source, so you can check them yourself.

The short answer first: scan statistics answer three questions, and only three. Which placement got attention? When did it get it? And on what kind of device and from roughly where? Everything else — whether a scan became a customer, whether the poster was good — is an interpretation you add on top, and most misreadings come from treating an interpretation as if it were a measurement.

What FreeQrly counts

Open a dynamic code in your Dashboard and you see the same set of numbers for every code. The definitions matter, because two of the most common misreadings come from confusing “total” with “unique” and “today” with “all time.”

NumberWhat it meansHow to read it
Total scansEvery scan of the code, all timeThe lifetime footprint of that code. Compare totals only between codes that have been out for the same length of time.
TodayScans so far todayA live pulse, not a verdict. A quiet morning says nothing about the evening.
Unique scansDistinct scanners, counted once per day. Two scans by the same person on the same day count as one unique scan.The closest thing to “how many people.” If total is far above unique, a small group is scanning repeatedly — or one code is being passed around.

Each breakdown comes with counts and percentages. Percentages are shares of that code's scans, not shares of anything larger — a code with mostly iOS scans does not mean most of your customers use iPhones. It means most of the people who scanned that code did so on an iPhone.

Two outside numbers, with their sources attached

This post deliberately cites very few outside numbers, and the reason is worth stating plainly: a QR statistic borrowed from a roundup usually arrives without the three facts that make a number usable — what was measured, over whom, and when. The two numbers below arrive with all three, from their original publishers.

The QR standard itself: how much damage a code can survive

Scan statistics are only half of a print decision; the other half is whether the code survives handling. The QR standard, ISO/IEC 18004:2024, defines four error-correction levels, each stating how much of the symbol's codewords can be recovered if damaged:

LevelRecoverable codewordsTypical use
L7%Clean digital display
M15%Everyday indoor print
Q25%Print that gets handled or lives outdoors
H30%Codes carrying a centre logo, or rough handling

Source: ISO/IEC 18004:2024, the QR code bar code symbology specification, via the ISO standard page (iso.org/standard/83389.html). Read the percentages carefully: they describe codewords, not printed surface area. A code at level H does not survive every possible 30% hole in the print, and a higher level buys resilience by spending more of the code on redundancy. For statistics, the practical point: a code printed at a low level on a handled surface will under-report its true audience — people tried to scan, the code failed, and no scan was recorded.

Who carries a camera phone: Pew's United States survey

When you read a device breakdown, it helps to know the ceiling: how many people in the audience could scan at all. Pew Research Center's 2026 mobile fact sheet reports that 91% of United States adults said they owned a smartphone, and 98% said they owned a cellphone of some kind, up from 35% smartphone ownership in the Center's first such survey in 2011. Source: Pew Research Center, “Americans' Use of Mobile Devices in 2026” (pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/), based on a survey of 5,511 United States adults conducted January 30 to June 18, 2026, with an overall margin of error of plus or minus 1.8 percentage points.

Three cautions travel with that number, and they apply to any outside figure you meet. First, population: this is United States adults, not the world, and not your event's guest list. Second, measure: self-reported ownership, not scanning behaviour. Third, date: a 2026 survey describes 2026. A number used outside its population, its measure, or its date stops being evidence and becomes decoration.

How to read your own numbers: five questions

Your Dashboard numbers need no outside benchmark to be useful. Run every reading through these five questions before acting on it:

  1. Compared with what? A code's total means little alone. Compare codes that ran in the same period, in similar placements, for the same offer. A flyer code and a poster code from the same week can be compared; a flyer code from last spring and a poster code from this week cannot, because the season, the offer, and the audience all changed between them.
  2. Over what period? The 7-day chart exists to stop single-day readings. A code that looks dead on a Monday may be an evening code, a weekend code, or a commuter-route code. Read the shape of the week before judging the height of a day.
  3. Total or unique? If total runs far ahead of unique, the same people are coming back — which is good for a menu or a timetable, and a warning sign for a one-time offer. If unique runs close to total, each scan is a different person, and the code is reaching broadly rather than deeply.
  4. What else happened that day? Spikes need context. A burst of scans right after a post goes out is the post, not the poster. A burst on a day you changed the destination is the new destination, not the old placement. Note external events — a post, a mention, a price change — beside the chart, or the chart will mislead you with perfect confidence.
  5. What did the scan lead to? A scan is a glance, not a conversion. Pair scan statistics with whatever the destination itself measures — form submissions, orders, sign-ups — and judge the code by the destination's outcome, not by the scan count alone.

Reading a real week: patterns, not just totals

The same breakdowns, read as behaviour rather than numbers:

  • Evening-only scans. A code that wakes up after work hours is probably on a commuter route or an evening venue — evening visitors, not office visitors.
  • A sudden new city. Scans spreading to a city you never printed in usually mean someone shared the code in a chat or a post — the code travelled without you. Check where the code is pointing: a shared code sends strangers to your destination, so the destination should make sense to a stranger.
  • Weekday-only scans. A code that goes quiet on weekends is probably on an office poster or a weekday venue, not a weekend one.
  • Desktop-heavy scans. A poster that gets desktop scans is probably being scanned from a screenshot or a forwarded image, not from the wall. The wall version may be fine; the screenshot version is doing the work, and the destination should be checked on a desktop screen as well as on a phone.
  • Zero is data. A code with no scans for a week is telling you the placement is wrong, not the code. Codes do not fail silently very often — a printed code either scans or it does not, and a quick test with your own phone settles that in seconds. If it scans and nobody scans it, the problem is position, size, contrast, or the absence of a reason to scan, in that order.

A worked example, with the arithmetic shown

Illustrative example — the numbers below are hypothetical, used only to show the arithmetic, not measurements from any real campaign. Suppose two codes point at the same page for the same two weeks. Code A, on a counter sign, records 120 total scans and 90 unique scans. Code B, on a window poster, records 60 total scans and 55 unique scans. Three readings follow, each with a different action:

  • Reach: Code A reached more distinct people (90 against 55), so the counter sign is the broader placement.
  • Repeat use: Code A's total runs 30 scans ahead of its unique count, while Code B's runs only 5 ahead, so counter visitors scan repeatedly while poster visitors mostly scan once. That fits a counter sign seen by regulars and a poster seen by passers-by — and it suggests the counter code's destination should reward a second visit, while the poster code's destination should make its case in one visit.
  • Next test: Swap nothing yet. Move Code B's poster to the counter for a week and Code A's sign to the window, then compare again. If the pattern follows the placement, the placement was the cause. If the pattern follows the code, the destination or the design was the cause. One swap beats a month of debate about which sign is better.

The discipline in that example is the point, not the numbers: same period, same destination, one change at a time, and a decision rule written before the test runs.

Set up a comparison you can trust

  1. Give each placement its own dynamic code. One code per placement — counter, window, flyer, receipt — all pointing at the same destination. Shared codes cannot tell placements apart, and a comparison built on a shared code is guesswork with a chart.
  2. Keep the destination identical. If two codes point at different pages, you are testing pages, not placements. Test one variable at a time.
  3. Run them for the same period. Start and end together, through the same weekdays and weekends. A code that ran over a holiday weekend is not comparable with one that ran over an ordinary week.
  4. Note what else happened. Keep a one-line log beside the chart: post published, price changed, venue closed. Spikes without notes become mysteries.
  5. Decide the action before you look. Write down what you will do if A outpulls B — move B, reprint B, retire B — before opening the statistics.
  6. Act on the result, then re-measure. Move the weak code, change the destination of the strong one, or retire the silent one — and watch the next 7-day chart for the change. A statistic that never changes a decision was entertainment, not measurement.

What FreeQrly never collects

Stats are aggregates — there is no per-visitor record to look at. A “unique scan” is a one-way hash of the scanner's IP address, browser, and date: enough to count distinct scanners per day, impossible to reverse into a person. No names, no emails, no exact locations, no cross-code tracking of individuals. And static codes track nothing at all — they never touch a server, so there is nothing to count.

That limit shapes what you should ask of the statistics. You can ask which placement won, when it won, and on what devices. You cannot ask who scanned: a scan carries no identity, and any identity attached to it came from somewhere else. If your decision genuinely needs to know who responded, put a form behind the code and let people identify themselves there, voluntarily.

What scan statistics cannot tell you

  • Scans are not customers. A scan is a glance. Someone can scan out of curiosity, by accident, or to show a friend, and none of those are a sale. Judge the destination's outcome, not the scan count.
  • Scans are not satisfaction. A heavily scanned code on a complaint form is not a success story. Read the destination's content alongside the counts.
  • Averages hide the shape. Two codes with the same weekly total can have opposite patterns — one steady, one spiky — and the steady one is usually the healthier placement. Always read the 7-day chart, not just the total.
  • Silence is ambiguous. Zero scans can mean a bad placement, a damaged print, a changed destination, or a code printed where nobody looks. Test the print with your own phone before concluding anything about the audience.
  • Outside benchmarks mislead more than they help. A figure quoted without its population, measure, and date cannot be compared with your codes, because you do not know what it counted. Vendor-published figures deserve an extra check: read them as the vendor's own account of its own users, and look for the methodology page that states who was surveyed and how. A figure whose original page states its method can be used, with its scope attached; a figure carried secondhand, stripped of its method, should be left out of your decision entirely. That is why this post leans on your own Dashboard and on two standards-grade sources rather than on a roundup of borrowed percentages.

A weekly routine that takes ten minutes

  • Open each live code and read the 7-day chart shape first, totals second.
  • Compare placements that ran side by side, same period, same destination.
  • Move, repoint, or retire the weakest placement — one change, not five.
  • Check the destination on the device most scanners used that week.
  • Note any external event beside the chart, so next week's spike has an explanation ready.

Do that weekly for a month and the statistics become a habit that moves signs and retires dead placements.

Good to know

What counts as a unique scan?

One scanner, once per day. FreeQrly counts distinct scanners using a one-way hash of IP address, browser, and date — the same person scanning twice in a day counts once.

Do static QR codes track scans?

No. Static codes never touch a server — the destination lives in the pattern — so there is nothing to count. Only dynamic codes report scans.

Is visitor data private?

Yes. Stats are aggregates only: counts by day, country, city, device, and OS. No names, emails, exact locations, or per-visitor records. Unique scans use a non-reversible hash.

Can I see who scanned my QR code?

No — and that is by design. FreeQrly counts scans; it does not identify scanners. A scan carries no identity, so if a decision needs to know who responded, put a form behind the code and let people identify themselves there voluntarily.

Why does one code show many more total scans than unique scans?

Because the same people are scanning repeatedly. A total far ahead of the unique count fits a code seen by regulars, such as a counter sign or a menu; a total close to the unique count fits a code seen mostly by passers-by. Compare the two numbers together — total alone hides repeat use.

My code shows zero scans — is it broken?

Not necessarily. First test the print with your own phone: if it scans, the problem is placement, size, contrast, or the absence of a reason to scan, in that order. Zero scans for a week is data about the placement, not proof the code failed. If it does not scan, check damage, contrast, and size before concluding anything about the audience.

How do I compare two placements fairly?

Give each placement its own dynamic code pointing at the same destination, run them for the same period through the same weekdays and weekends, and decide the action before you look — what you will do if one outpulls the other. Note anything else that happened that week, such as a post going out, beside the chart.

Why does this post cite so few outside statistics?

Because an outside figure is only usable with its measure, its population, and its date attached. This post cites two figures that arrive with all three from their original publishers — the ISO QR error-correction levels and Pew's United States smartphone survey — and leans on your own Dashboard for the rest.
