Get more Google reviews with a QR code

The best time to ask for a review is the moment a happy customer is standing in front of you. A QR code makes that moment one scan long.

October 19, 2026 · Last updated: November 3, 2026 · 15 min read

Google reviews decide who gets picked — and the businesses with the most reviews did not get them by luck. They asked, at the right moment, with zero friction. A QR code pointing straight at your Google review form turns “please leave us a review” into a single scan. Get the link Google itself issues, turn it into a code, put it where a satisfied customer is already standing, and ask once, plainly. Everything else in this guide is detail around that sequence.

The reason a code works where a follow-up email often does not is timing. A request that arrives days later competes with everything else in an inbox. A code on the table, the receipt, or the card handed over at the end of a visit meets the customer while the experience is still fresh. The code does not persuade anyone. It removes the steps between “I would review this place” and the review form: no searching for the business name, no picking the right listing among lookalikes, no hunting for the review button.

Step 1 — get your Google review link

You need the direct link to your review form, not just your business listing. Google issues this link from your Business Profile, and its current help page gives these steps:

  1. On a computer, go to your Business Profile while signed in to the Google account that manages it.
  2. Select Read reviews, then Get more reviews.
  3. Select Copy to copy the review link. The link looks like g.page/r/…/review — a short link that opens your review form directly.

Two details matter here. First, the same panel also holds a QR code you can save by right-clicking it and choosing “Save image as” — but Google notes that review QR codes can only be generated on a computer browser, not on a mobile device, so do this step at a desk. Second, Google's own code is plain: no branding, no frame, no instruction line. For a counter sign or table tent, generating a styled code from the same link — logo, frame, and a line telling people what happens when they scan — is usually worth the extra minute. The destination is identical either way.

Test the link before anything goes to print. Open it in a private browser window and confirm it lands on your business's review form, not on a general listing or a similarly named business. A printed code with the wrong destination teaches customers that your codes lead nowhere useful.

Step 2 — turn it into a QR code

Paste the link into a Google review QR code generator — it takes seconds, it is free, and a static code never expires. Print it at a decent size, keep dark ink on a light background, and leave a clear quiet margin around the code. The print size guide covers sizing in detail; the short version is that a code people have to lean in to read is a code people will not scan.

Now choose between static and dynamic, because the choice is harder to reverse once hundreds of cards are printed:

  • Static is the default for most shops. The review link lives inside the pattern itself, the code needs no account, and it keeps working as long as your Business Profile exists. One static code, printed well and placed well, is all most single-site businesses need.
  • Dynamic earns its keep in two situations. First, when the destination might change: if Google ever changes your review link, or you move the request to a different page, a dynamic code lets you change the destination from your Dashboard without reprinting. Second, when you want to compare placements: give the counter, the receipt, and the delivery bag each their own dynamic code, and the scan statistics show which placement actually gets scanned. Dynamic codes are free to start with a sign-in, editable after printing, and come with scan statistics, with 20 codes on the free account and unlimited scans during the limited-time promotion. The trade-off is honest: a dynamic code points at a short link on a server, so it depends on that service staying up, while a static code depends on nothing but the printed pattern and the destination.

If you run several locations, do not share one code across them. Each location has its own Business Profile and its own review link, so make one code per location, label the files by location name, and check each one before printing.

Step 3 — put it where the moment happens

The code has to be in front of a satisfied customer before they leave. Work through your own customer journey and mark every point where someone is happy, unhurried, and holding their phone — those are your placements, and everywhere else is decoration.

Restaurants and cafés

Table tents and the receipt are the two placements that matter. The table tent catches people mid-meal, when they are relaxed and the phone is already on the table. The receipt catches them at the end, when the whole visit is fresh. A code on the front door, by contrast, is scanned by people arriving — the one moment when they have nothing to review yet. Match the wording to the moment: “Enjoyed your meal? One scan.”

Retail and salons

The checkout counter is the natural spot — satisfaction peaks at purchase, and the customer is already standing there with their phone in hand while the card payment goes through. Put the code at eye level, not flat on the counter where bags cover it, and not behind the till where only staff can see it. A small standing sign next to the card reader outperforms a poster on the far wall.

Services: plumbers, tutors, clinics, salons

For work done at a customer's home or in an appointment, the handover moment is everything. A card handed over at the end of the visit — or a sticker on the invoice — travels home and sits on the kitchen counter as a reminder. The spoken ask matters as much as the card: “If you were happy with the work, this card takes you straight to our Google reviews.” Without that sentence, the card is just another piece of paper.

Delivery and takeaway

A sticker on the bag or box puts the code in front of the customer at the exact moment they open the food. The food is the reminder, and the code is already in their hands. Keep the wording to one line: a delivery bag has limited space and a short attention window.

Email signatures and follow-up messages

For businesses that already email customers — invoices, booking confirmations, thank-you notes — the signature is a quiet, permanent placement. Add the review link as a short text line rather than a code image, since the reader is already on a screen. The same applies to a follow-up message after the visit: one short message, one link, sent the same day while the visit is still fresh.

What to write next to it

A bare code gets fewer scans than a code with a reason. Keep it short and specific:

  • “Love your visit? Scan to review us on Google.”
  • “Enjoyed your meal? One scan to review us.”
  • “Happy with the job? This code takes you to our Google reviews.”

Asking for Google reviews by name matters — customers default to whichever platform is easiest. Wording that hints at a star rating (“Leave us five stars”) crosses from asking into steering, and Google's policy treats steering as a violation, as the compliance section below explains.

The compliance rules, verified against Google's own pages

This section only states policy points that appear on Google's official support pages, because review policy is exactly the kind of topic where secondhand summaries drift. Google's prohibited and restricted content page and its tips for getting more reviews page agree on the following:

  • Reviews must reflect a genuine experience, and be genuine and unbiased. That is the foundation everything else hangs from. A review from someone who never visited, or a review written at the business's dictation, fails this test no matter how it was obtained.
  • Never offer incentives for a review. Google does not allow merchants to offer payment, discounts, free goods, or free services in exchange for posting a review, revising a review, or removing a negative review. Incentivized or biased reviews are removed from Maps. This covers the obvious cases — a discount for a review, a free dessert for a review — and the less obvious ones, like an entry into a prize draw for reviewers or a benefit offered to take a negative review down.
  • Do not discourage negative reviews, and do not ask only the customers you expect to be happy. Google's policy lists both as rating manipulation: discouraging or prohibiting negative reviews, and selectively soliciting positive reviews. The compliant version is simple and also better business: ask every customer, the same way, at the same point in the journey. A review profile built only from hand-picked happy customers is both a policy risk and a credibility problem — readers can tell.
  • Do not require or pressure people to review while they are on the premises, and do not script the content. Google's policy says merchants should not require or pressure users to leave ratings or write reviews while on the premises, and should not request that specific content be included — including staff quotas or reviews mentioning a particular staff member. The practical line: one visible code plus one spoken, voluntary ask is compliant; standing over a customer until they post is not.
  • Asking itself is allowed. Google expressly allows merchants to solicit or encourage content that does represent a genuine experience, provided no incentive is offered and no attempt is made to influence the rating or the contents. The QR code is simply the lowest-friction form of that allowed ask.

Beyond Google's own rules, United States businesses should know that the Federal Trade Commission's Consumer Reviews rule, in effect since October 21, 2024, prohibits providing compensation or other incentives conditioned on a review expressing a particular sentiment, and authorizes civil penalties for knowing violations. Note the difference in scope: the FTC rule targets incentives tied to a sentiment, while Google's wording bars incentives for posting a review at all.

Compliance is not just about avoiding removal. A business that answers its reviews gets more of them, because reviewers can see they are read. Google's tips page advises keeping replies professional, polite, short, and specific rather than sending the same thank-you to everyone, and moving a complex complaint to a private channel rather than arguing in public. Reply to the negative reviews too, calmly and without sharing the reviewer's private information.

The honest comparison: what a review code cannot do

A review code is a friction remover, not a reputation machine, and it is worth stating its limits plainly before you print a hundred cards:

  • It cannot create satisfaction. A code in front of an unhappy customer produces, at best, nothing, and at worst an honest negative review. Fix the experience first.
  • It cannot replace asking. The code is passive. In every placement above, it works best paired with one spoken sentence at the right moment, and then silence.
  • It cannot target only happy customers. Any system that filters — a code shown only after a good outcome, a request sent only to customers who smiled — breaks Google's rule against selective solicitation. Ask everyone and reply to all of it.
  • It cannot fix a broken link by itself. A static code holds the link it was printed with. If your review link changes, a static code keeps pointing at the old destination. That is the one case where a dynamic code pays for itself: change the destination once in the Dashboard and every printed copy follows.

A few honest tips

  • Ask, don't beg. One visible code plus a spoken “we'd love a review” outperforms five signs.
  • Start with one placement, not five. Put the code where your happiest moment already happens and run it for a few weeks before printing it everywhere.
  • Reply to reviews. A business that answers reviews gets more of them; reviewers can see you read them. Keep replies short, specific, and human.
  • Track what works. If you want to know which placement drives scans, use a dynamic code for one location and compare.

Good to know

How do I create a QR code for Google reviews?

Copy your Google review link from your Business Profile (Read reviews, then Get more reviews, then Copy), paste it into a free QR code generator, and print the code. Static codes are free and never expire. Note that Google generates its own review QR code only on a computer browser, not on a mobile device.

Where should I put my Google review QR code?

Wherever a satisfied customer stands before leaving: restaurant tables and receipts, retail checkout counters, service invoices, or delivery packaging. For email, use the review link as a clickable text line in your signature instead of a code image, since the reader is already on a screen.

Do Google review QR codes expire?

A static QR code never expires — it holds your review link directly. As long as your Google Business Profile exists, the code keeps working. A dynamic code keeps working too, as long as the service behind its short link stays up and you do not delete the code.

Should I offer a discount for a Google review?

No. Google prohibits offering payment, discounts, free goods, or free services in exchange for posting, revising, or removing a review, and incentivized or biased reviews are removed from Maps. In the United States, the FTC Consumer Reviews rule also prohibits incentives conditioned on a review expressing a particular sentiment. Ask at the right moment instead — that is what the QR code is for.

Should I use a static or a dynamic code for Google reviews?

Static for most single-site businesses: free, no sign-up, never expires. Dynamic when the destination might change or when you want to compare placements — give the counter, the receipt, and the delivery bag each their own dynamic code and compare their scan statistics. Dynamic codes are free with a sign-in, editable after printing, and come with scan statistics.

Can I ask customers for a review while they are still in my shop?

Yes, with one spoken, voluntary ask — but do not require or pressure anyone to review while on the premises, and do not hand them wording to copy. Google's policy bars requiring or pressuring users to leave ratings or reviews on the premises and bars requesting specific content, including staff review quotas.

What if my Google review link changes after I printed cards?

A static code keeps pointing at the old link, so you would need to reprint. A dynamic code can be repointed in the Dashboard without reprinting: change the destination once and every printed copy follows. If you print static codes, keep a note of which link each batch carries and re-test the link after any change to your Business Profile.
