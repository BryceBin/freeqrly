QR Codes for Breweries: What a Guest Actually Scans, From the Door to the Fridge at Home

Put a QR code everywhere a guest has a question and a phone in their hand — what's pouring, what's in this beer, what's on tonight, what the food truck is serving, how to take it home. The only decision that really matters is which codes must stay editable after printing.

November 4, 2026 · Last updated: November 19, 2026 · 17 min read

Anything that changes weekly — the tap list, the event lineup, the food-truck schedule — needs a dynamic code you can update without reprinting. Anything that never changes — guest WiFi, the brewery story, a permanent tour stop — can be a static code.

A brewery is not a shop with a fixed catalogue: a keg kicks at 7 PM, a small-batch sour sells out before the food truck arrives, a band cancels and trivia takes its place. This guide follows one guest's night in the taproom, then one beer's life from brew day to the fridge at home.

Almost everything here is dynamic, because almost everything in a taproom changes. And every code needs a plain-language label — the label, not the code, earns the scan.

Act one: the door — tonight's taps before they sit down

The first scan happens before the guest orders anything. They are standing in the doorway, deciding whether this is the place for tonight, and the question is simple: what's pouring, and is anything happening? A code on the door, the window, or the A-board should answer exactly that — tonight's taps, today's hours, tonight's event — and nothing else.

This is a dynamic code: when trivia replaces a cancelled band, you update the destination, not the poster. One person per shift owns the update. A dynamic code removes the reprint, not the need to update.

Act two: the bar — the tap list that never lies

The tap list is why most breweries reach for QR codes first. A printed tap list is wrong the moment a keg kicks. Print the code once — on table tents, the bar top, a wall board, the flight paddle — and point it at a page showing every pouring beer with five things: name, style, ABV, pour sizes with prices, and a clear sold-out state. The sold-out state matters most: mark a kicked keg as sold out immediately, instead of deleting the beer silently. A guest who can see a beer is gone chooses another beer.

A rotating small batch is why the code must be dynamic: breweries that release a new experimental beer every few weeks often give it a batch number, and the tap-list page carries the story of what went into it and how long it will last. The flight paddle works the same way on a smaller scale: a code on the paddle can open the tasting notes for exactly the four beers on it.

Act three: the glass in their hand — Untappd and the rating moment

Nobody rates a beer in the abstract. They rate the glass in front of them, in the first few sips, while the impression is fresh. That is the moment a QR code should catch — and Untappd is where most of that rating happens. A small code on the table card, flight card, or coaster can open the Untappd page for the exact beer the guest is drinking, so checking in takes one scan instead of a search. One code per beer is the right granularity: a guest drinking the hazy IPA should land on the hazy IPA's page, not the brewery's general profile.

Three rules keep this honest. Ask for the check-in, not for a score. "Rate this beer on Untappd" is a fair ask; asking for five stars, or only handing the card to guests who are smiling, is not. Do not pay for ratings. No free pour, discount, or prize draw entry in exchange for a rating — bought praise is noise. Read the low scores like a brewer. If the same complaint shows up three times — too sweet, too thin, over-carbonated — that is the most useful feedback the taproom will produce all month. Put the code on the flat card next to the glass, not on curved glass that will not scan.

A dynamic code behind each beer also shows which beers guests look up, not just which ones sell — treat those counts as a trend over weeks, not a verdict on one night.

Act four: the table — event nights that fill the room on a Tuesday

A taproom's hardest problem is not Friday. It is Tuesday. Event nights exist to solve Tuesday, and a QR code exists to make the event findable after the poster goes up. Trivia, live music, and new-release nights each need a slightly different code, because each asks the guest to do a different thing. A trivia code opens the night's details — start time, team size, how to join — and, once the night starts, the answer sheet or score form. A live music code opens the artist, the set time, and the cover charge. A new-release night code opens the release itself — what the beer is, what it costs, whether cans are available — and a pre-order link turns a tasting into a take-home sale.

Act five: the truck outside — food-truck tie-ins and pairings

Many taprooms have no kitchen. They have a truck, or a rotation of trucks, parked outside — a second menu that changes even faster than the tap list. The guest's question is always the same: what is that truck serving, and what beer goes with it? A code at the door, on the table tent, and on the truck's window should open tonight's food menu with an ordering link, if the truck takes orders by phone. This is a dynamic code, updated every time the truck changes.

The pairing note is the part worth writing properly: a short line under each food item naming the beer it suits. Match intensity first, then complement, contrast, or cut richness with carbonation and bitterness. For a fictional taproom with a rotating truck (SAMPLE DATA):

Act six: last call — to-go orders, cans, and gift cards

The last scan of the night is the one that makes money after the guest leaves. A guest who loved a beer wants to take it home, and the moment to ask is while they are still holding the glass — not the next morning, when they have forgotten the name. A code on the bar, on the till, and on the takeout bag should open the to-go page: which cans and crowlers are available tonight, what they cost, and how to order. To-go must be dynamic: can availability changes daily. Keep the page short: beer, size, price, order button.

Gift cards deserve a code of their own, on the merch shelf and the takeout bag. The buyer is not the drinker, and needs to know what the card covers before paying: amounts, what it can be used for, and whether it expires. Crowlers — large cans filled and sealed at the bar — are the to-go item most worth a code: a crowler has no label beyond what the bar writes on it, so a code on the crowler can carry the beer's name, style, ABV, tasting notes, and the date it was filled.

The beer after it leaves: merch that keeps working

Match the destination's lifespan to the item's lifespan: a can, drunk within weeks, can point to that specific release — its tasting notes, its story, the food it pairs with. A glass or hoodie, used for years, should point to something durable — the homepage, the online shop, or the events page, kept current through a dynamic code. A glass that points at a release from two years ago advertises a dead beer. A gift card should point to the gift-card page; a sticker to the brewery story or tap list.

One clear code with a plain label beats three unexplained squares. On a can, leave the legally required label information on the label itself and let the code add detail. On a shirt, put the code where it can be scanned flat, not across a seam, and test it on the finished garment — fabric moves, and a code printed across a fold will not scan.

Regulars and the mug club: loyalty without an app

Mug clubs live or die on friction. The classic version — a numbered mug on a shelf and a paper list behind the bar — works until it grows, and then it becomes a chore for staff and a mystery for members. A QR code does not run the club, but it removes the two most common friction points: joining, and knowing what members get. Put a code on the bar, the mug shelf, and the member card that opens one page: how to join, what membership includes, member nights, and how to claim a perk.

Signup has to be where the regular already is, at the moment they are enjoying the beer. A signup form behind a code on the back of a coaster — something a guest keeps rather than throws away — works because the guest finds it in their hand at exactly the right time. A dynamic membership page lets you update perks and prices without reprinting. Keep the QR code as the front door, not the whole system: it should get a guest to signup or a perk in one scan, not demand an app download.

Reviews at the right moment — and the line not to cross

A QR code makes leaving a review easy: instead of asking a guest to search for your name and find the review button, a scan takes them directly to the review form. The link comes from your Google Business Profile (Read reviews, then Get more reviews). Put the code on the bill folder, the takeout bag, or a small table card — never pushed at a guest mid-meal. Word it plainly — "Share an honest review on Google" — and ask every guest, not only those who praised the beer.

The line matters. Google prohibits incentives in exchange for reviews, and also prohibits asking only happy customers. Never offer a discount, a free pour, or a prize for a review, and never ask for a particular star rating or wording. An honest review saying the stout was too thin tells you what to fix; ten bought five-star reviews tell you nothing.

The wet, dim, loud reality: printing a code that still scans

A taproom is a hard place for a QR code: dim light, condensation, greasy hands, curved glasses, guests scanning at arm's length or across a bar. The rule of thumb is 10:1: make the code at least one tenth as wide as the scan distance. A code read from 25 centimetres should be about 2.5 centimetres wide; a poster read from 3 metres needs about 30 centimetres. Never go below about 2 by 2 centimetres in print, and make denser codes larger.

PlacementScan distanceMinimum code widthTaproom hazard to beat
Coaster or flight card20–30 cm2–3 cmCondensation rings, wet hands
Table tent or menu30–50 cm3–5 cmLow light, clutter, leaning glasses
Wall poster or window1–2 m10–20 cmGlare from glass and sun

Four more rules. Leave a quiet zone: a blank margin at least four modules wide on all four sides. Print dark on light, and prefer matte: gloss and foil reflect light straight into the camera. Print for water: paper tents will not survive wipe-downs and spills, so use vinyl or polyester stock, or laminate with a sealed edge, and pigment-based or laser printing rather than dye-based ink, which smears when wet. Export sharp, test real: export SVG where your printer accepts it, or PNG at 300 pixels per inch, and test a proof at real size with an iPhone and an Android phone, in the real lighting. A dynamic code can rescue a wrong destination after printing; it cannot rescue a code that is too small, too low-contrast, or physically damaged.

The one decision that decides your reprint bill: static or dynamic

A static code stores its data directly in the pattern itself. Once printed, that data does not change. A dynamic code stores a short redirect instead, so the destination can be edited without replacing the printed pattern, and scans can be counted. The test: could you replace every copy of this code if the destination changed tomorrow? If not, print a code whose destination can change.

A rollout plan for one brewery: four weeks, no big print run

Keep each week's print run small until the codes are proven. Week 1 — the tap list: one dynamic code for the live tap list, on table tents and the bar top. Week 2 — the door and events: a dynamic code for tonight's event page on the door poster, starting with Tuesday trivia. Week 3 — the glass and the truck: a rating code for each pouring beer, a dynamic code for the food-truck menu, and the to-go page code at the till. Week 4 — the regulars and the review: the mug-club signup code, the review code, and the WiFi code where guests sit down.

Then watch the scan counts for a month: codes that get scanned earn better placement; codes that don't get removed. Keep a list of your codes — where each is printed, what it points to, static or dynamic — so one person can update the right destination in minutes. Fewer, clearer codes beat a wall of squares guests learn to ignore. Print once, update often, and let the beer do the rest.

Good to know

Should a brewery tap list use a dynamic or a static QR code?

Dynamic. Tap lists, prices, and sold-out states change constantly, and a dynamic code lets you update the linked page without reprinting. A static code would need a full reprint every time a keg kicks.

What should a tap-list page show for each beer?

Name, style, ABV, pour sizes with prices, and a clear sold-out state. Mark a kicked keg as sold out immediately, rather than deleting the beer silently.

Do I need a different QR code for each beer?

Not for the tap list — one code that opens the full list is simpler. Use a separate code per beer only where one beer genuinely needs its own page, most commonly its Untappd rating page or a limited-release page.

Can a QR code take to-go and gift-card orders?

Yes. A code at the bar or till can open a to-go page with tonight's cans, crowlers, and prices — that one must be dynamic, because can availability changes daily. A code on the merch shelf or takeout bag can open the gift-card page, which rarely changes, so a static code works for that one.

Can I offer a free drink for a Google review?

No. Google prohibits incentives in exchange for reviews and selectively asking only happy customers. Ask every guest for an honest review, never request a particular star rating or wording, and never offer a discount, a free pour, or a prize for a review.

How big should a QR code be on a coaster or table tent?

Use the 10:1 rule: make the code at least one tenth as wide as the scan distance — about 2 to 3 centimetres for a coaster scanned from 20 to 30 centimetres, and never below about 2 by 2 centimetres in print.

How do I make a taproom QR code survive spills and condensation?

Print on vinyl or polyester stock, or laminate with a sealed edge, and use pigment-based or laser printing rather than dye-based ink, which smears when wet. Prefer matte over gloss, keep a four-module quiet zone, and test a proof at real size with an iPhone and an Android phone before the full run.

Which brewery QR codes can be static?

Anything whose destination will never change: the guest WiFi login, the permanent brewery-history page, and a fixed tour route. Static codes are free with FreeQrly, need no sign-up, carry no watermark, and never expire. When in doubt, choose dynamic.
